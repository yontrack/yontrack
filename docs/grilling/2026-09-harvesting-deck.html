Why we are here

Four questions, asked about the present and the past

How fast does a change reach production?

Lead time from build to deployment, deployment frequency, success rate, time to recover from a failed deployment.

Can the tests be trusted?

Share of builds whose test stages all pass, and how often a build flips from fail to pass without a change.

Is security scanning real?

Not "do we have a scanner" but: every expected kind of scan, fresh, and actually gating a release.

How long does a HIGH stay open?

Time from first sighting of a critical or high vulnerability to its disappearance, against a target the organization declared.

None of these questions can be answered without data. Yet in most organizations the maturity of software delivery is assessed from interviews and impressions: the numbers that would settle it are scattered across CI servers, scanners, spreadsheets and release emails, and nobody has ever put them side by side.

The questions2 / 25
Problem 1 of 4 · maturity

Nobody can say how mature delivery is

  • Maturity is a feeling, not a reading. There is no view across projects. The numbers that exist live in people's heads and in slide decks, and each team measures itself with its own yardstick.
  • The tools that exist measure the wrong thing, or export it somewhere nobody looks. Delivery metrics computed over promotions rather than deployments, then pushed to a time-series database that is off by default: the right measures, invisible from where decisions are made.
  • Unmeasured looks the same as failing. A maturity model that normalises everything to a 0–100 score and an A–F rating gives a blind spot the exact shape of a bad score. For an estate report built on patchy data, that is fatal: half the value is knowing where we cannot see.

What "reading it" has to mean

  • Across projects, not one page at a time
  • Over time, with a stated window
  • Computed from delivery history, never typed in
  • Able to say unknown, distinct from bad
  • Able to say estimated, distinct from measured
Problems3 / 25
Problem 2 of 4 · access and sense

The data is hard to reach, and harder to make sense of

Reaching it

  • Sources are the usual four, SCM, CI, trackers, scanners, plus the messy human ones: spreadsheets, wikis, release emails.
  • Each lives behind its own access, in its own format, with its own retention.
  • Credentials are often not granted. What we get is an export somebody ran for us, handed over as a file.
  • Nothing outside the organization can reach in, and little inside can reach out.

Making sense of it

  • Every team has its own vocabulary for the same pipeline: job names, stage names, what "released" means.
  • One build is seen three ways: a git tag, a Jenkins run, a spreadsheet row saying "release 3.4 shipped Monday". Nothing joins them.
  • "Deployed" is frequently recorded nowhere. Lead time to production has no end point to measure to.
  • The history is decaying while we plan. CI keeps weeks; scanners keep less; git keeps years. Next slide.

The rule this sets

Harvest by durability, not by ease of access. Snapshot the perishable sources early and coarsely, before the mapping is even designed. Reconstruct the rest from the durable sources later, at leisure.

A git-only estate, with no CI history left, is the common case this predicts. The design below treats it as normal, not as a failure.

Problems5 / 25
Problem 3 of 4 · vulnerabilities

CVEs are counted, then forgotten

  • A scan reaches the dashboard as four integers: critical, high, medium, low. The scanner's UNKNOWN severity has no slot and is dropped. The same CVE in two packages counts twice.
  • A release report can say "5.3.1 had 3 HIGH" and "5.3.4 had 1 HIGH", and cannot say which two were fixed, or when. Remediation time is unmeasurable by construction.
  • Accepted risk lives in files read by bash. An ignore file for the image scanner, a suppressions file for the DAST scan: each carries a statement and an expiry that nothing enforces, and that no report reflects.
  • One CVE across forty projects: thirty fixed it, who is left and for how long? Unaskable today, because both the finding and the affected component dissolve into a count.

Today's pattern

scan count C / H / M / L forget

Every new scanner repeats it. Worth fixing even if no organization is ever harvested: any CI pipeline already produces exactly this data, and throws all but four integers away.

Problems6 / 25
Problem 4 of 4 · deployments

Deployments: the record that often does not exist

  • In most organizations there is no deployment record. A promotion, a tag, or a spreadsheet row is the closest thing to "it went live".
  • "Deployed" means something different in every team. A pipeline stage, a ticket closed, an email sent. Nothing agrees on the moment.
  • So the two measures that matter most have no end point. Lead time to production and time to recover both need the moment a build reached an environment, and it is the moment least likely to be recorded.
  • What is recorded does not replay. Deployment tools keep the current state, not the history; when a record exists it is a log line, gone in weeks. Lead time then gets measured to a promotion at best.

What the design has to provide

  • A deployment marker per estate: a real environment when one exists, otherwise a named promotion level
  • A way to record a deployment that happened in 2024, with its real start and end
  • Readings that compute from that marker, so the same definition of lead time holds for a harvested estate and a live one

Why it is the right place to assess delivery

  • It already holds the facts behind the four questions: when a build appeared, when it was promoted, what its tests and scans said, when and where it was deployed.
  • Every fact is timestamped and kept per build, so the past is queryable, not only the present.
  • Validations carry typed data, a test summary or a scan report, not a pass/fail flag; builds are linked across projects.

What it offers today

  • Fed by CI pipelines, a command-line tool and an API.
  • Read through project and branch pages, dashboards, delivery charts and notifications.
  • Environments and deployment pipelines, with admission rules and workflows.
Yontrack8 / 25
Yontrack · at the organization

Why it cannot simply be plugged in

  • The organization's CI/CD cannot push anywhere. Outbound HTTPS may exist; fully disconnected must work too.
  • Yontrack may not be installable inside the organization at all.
  • The history predates any integration. Nobody was posting builds to anything in 2024, and the readings need 2024.
  • Much of the history is not in a system. A spreadsheet row and a release email are facts too, with a lower confidence.
  • Yontrack itself expects the present. A build is created "now", a deployment starts "now", and every new fact fires notifications.

What we need

  • A way to bring in history Yontrack did not witness, from wherever it survives, as a file.
  • A Yontrack that accepts the past without disturbing the present: real dates, no notifications, no today's rules applied to 2024.
  • Vulnerabilities as things with a history, not counts.
  • A reading across an estate, with every number saying how sure it is.

The ledger is the seam. Collectors run inside the organization and write an append-only, versioned file of delivery facts with provenance. A separate importer replays it. Transport is deliberately dumb: HTTPS and a USB key are two shippers over one artifact.

Yontrack owns the contract. It publishes the JSON schema and accepts the upload, as it already does for configuration-as-code. This is history-as-code. If the format drifted to the harvester side, Yontrack would accept whatever it is sent and lose its second producer.

Two alternatives were rejected as primary: an in-org push gateway (presumes egress, nothing to inspect when numbers look wrong) and an edge Yontrack that federates (real product work, kept as the strategic direction if the pattern recurs).

Approach10 / 25
Approach · principles

Six principles the whole chain is held to

Portable ledger, dumb transport

Facts are collected where they are and moved as a file. No component assumes the network, and everything that can be inspected is a file on disk.

Yontrack owns the contract

The ledger schema is published by the instance, versioned by one integer, and validated at the door. Producers pin it as a fixture; a contract only one producer has written is a private format.

Agents produce configuration and rules, never numbers

They inventory the estate, draft the mapping, mine the human sources, review reconciliation. Their output is reviewable config in git, executed deterministically. Judgement enters as an estimated fact with evidence.

Harvest by durability

Fetch the perishable exports now, map them whenever the mapping is ready. Reconstruct from git later. Over-collecting is harmless because the server dedupes.

Readings are derived, never pushed

No REST update of a score, no computer interrogating another system. Import an organization's history and its scorecard exists, with nobody feeding a second system.

Unknown is a value, not a zero

Every reading carries its basis, measured, estimated or unknown, the window it covers and the branches that fed it. The estate view can be read measured-only.

Approach11 / 25
Inside the organization · harvester

The harvester: three inputs, two collectors, one file out

Inputs

  • Live collectors fetch a CI or an SCM into an export file, then read it.
  • Report collectors read the same export when someone ran the command for us. No credential needed.
  • Unstructured sources (spreadsheets, wikis, emails) are mined by the agent into the facts file, as estimated.

The mapping

  • One YAML per organization, validated against a published schema.
  • An explicit project list, drafted by the agent, reviewed by a human.
  • Stamp and level mappings organization-wide, with per-project overrides.
  • A deployment marker and a durability hint per source. Credentials by reference only.

Output

  • One ledger per run, all sources.
  • A reconciliation report beside it: builds seen by one source and not another, unmapped jobs, stale sources. Errors fail the run.
  • Builds joined across sources by commit; disagreements are listed, not smoothed over.
  • Incremental by watermark; the server deduplicates anyway.

Own repository, one static binary, one-shot commands run by the organization's own cron. Never a daemon.

Illustrative shape. The schema is published by the Yontrack instance.

The rules

  • A stream of facts, not a snapshot. One thing that happened, at one time, applied in order.
  • Every fact carries an id, a kind, a time, a basis and its evidence.
  • Deduplicated by source and id. Ids derive from the natural key, so re-collecting is harmless.
  • Thirteen kinds, closed: from project and branch down to build, validation, promotion, deployment.
  • One integer version; older ledgers are always accepted.
  • No corrections: purge the source and re-import.

Inside the organization · the agent

Where the agent sits, and where it does not

Agent proposeson a branch of the harvest repository
Human reviewsthe diff, then merges
Harvester runsdeterministically, from files on disk

Four procedures, written for any agent runtime and runnable by a human with no agent at all: inventory the sources and propose the project list; draft the mapping; mine facts from a spreadsheet or wiki page into the facts file, with the document as evidence; review the reconciliation report.

Guardrails that make the principle mechanical

  • The agent runs collect freely and never fetch against a live source. Credentials stay with a human or cron.
  • The harvester never holds a model key. The model belongs to the organization's agent runtime; judgement never sits inside the pipeline that produces numbers.
  • Anything the agent writes that is not a rule enters as estimated, with the source document as evidence. An imprecise date ("2024-03") is expanded, and the original precision kept in the evidence.
  • Ids are readable slugs (facts:release-3.4-shipped, jenkins:job/app-build/42), never UUIDs: a reconciliation report is debugged by a human reading ids.

Into Yontrack · import

The import: history arrives without waking anyone up

Validated at the door

The whole file is schema-checked in the request. A malformed ledger is rejected where the producer is standing; slow application runs behind.

Dry run, first-class

What would be created, skipped or rejected, and nothing written. The inspection a push gateway could never offer.

Applied in order

Facts sorted by time per project. A later ledger may carry older facts, harmlessly.

Quiet

Years of history must not fire years of notifications. Events are suppressed, model side effects kept, one "history arrived" event per run.

Below today's rules

A promotion rule configured in 2026 has nothing to say about 2024. Backfilled promotions and deployments are recorded as they happened.

Recorded, labelled, purgeable

An import run with counts and errors; a failing fact does not fail the run. Touched projects carry the source's label, and a source can be purged whole.

Behind a dedicated permission, and executed as the uploader so per-project rights hold. Import is a privilege because it can write dates.

  • The door is a scan report on a validation run: scanner, kind, list of findings. The ledger imports a scan through the same door.
  • Severity: critical, high, medium, low or unknown, with the scanner's raw word kept.

What becomes answerable

  • Resolution per branch, rolled up per project. "Fixed on main, still shipping on release/5.3" becomes askable.
  • Acceptance is in the model, read from the scanner's ignore files with their statement and expiry. An expired acceptance reopens the finding. Accepted is a third count, never "closed".
  • Remediation time: first seen to first absent, against the estate's target.
  • Fan-out. One CVE, forty projects, thirty fixed: who is left, on which branches, since when.
  • Two events: a new finding, a resolved one. "New HIGH on main" is the first notification anyone sets up.

Into Yontrack · readings

Readings: a fixed catalogue, derived from the model

ReadingShipsDefinition
Lead time6.0Build creation to the deployment marker.
Deployment frequency6.0Markers reached per window.
Success rate6.0Successful deployments over attempts.
Time to recover6.0Failed deployment to the next successful one.
Test stability6.0Share of builds with all tests passing, and fail-then-pass flips.
Security maturity6.1Ladder: none, reported, covered and fresh, gating a release.
Remediation6.1Median time to close critical and high findings; count open past target.

How a reading is taken

  • Materialised daily over a trailing window, 90 days by default. Retention purges the inputs, and forty projects must not mean forty live computations.
  • Keyed by estate. The marker, expected scans and targets are estate configuration, so one project can read differently in two estates.
  • Each reading records its window, its basis (measured / estimated / unknown), the reason when unknown, and the branches it read.
  • Branch rule fixed per reading, never a hardcoded name.
  • Fixed catalogue. Any addition is an issue against a real use case.

What an estate is

  • A named group of projects, selected by labels, with its deployment marker, the scans it expects, and its targets: days to close a critical, days to close a high.
  • Targets live on the estate. A project in two estates may be green in one and red in the other. The expectation belongs to whoever is reading.
  • No "all projects" estate. An estate must be declared to be read, which forces its targets to exist.
  • An imported organization is an estate the moment it lands.

Questions this answers

  • Was this lead time measured or estimated, over which window?
  • Where are we blind, and why?
  • Which projects still ship this CVE, on which branches, since when?
  • How long do highs stay open, against the target?
  • What was accepted rather than fixed, and until when?

What an auditor can re-run

  • A dry run of any ledger, without writing.
  • A re-collect from the same exports: same ids, same facts.
  • The reconciliation report: disagreements listed, not smoothed over.
  • The estate view measured-only: every estimate removed.

Delivery · what ships where

Four blocks, three release homes

Yontrack 6.0 major

  • The finding and observation model, in 6.0 so history accumulates from the first install.
  • The delivery readings and test stability; the project scorecard and security sections.

Yontrack 6.1 minor

  • Security maturity and remediation, once weeks of findings exist.
  • Estates and the estate view with fan-out.
  • The ledger contract, schema first, then the ledger import.
  • Yontrack learns to accept the past: quiet import, promotions below today's rules, deployments with a real start and end.

Harvester own cadence

  • Own repository, depending only on the published schema.
  • Tool, harvest-repository template, agent playbook.
  • Collectors for the organization's structured sources: SCM, CI, scanners.
  • Agents gather the rest: inventory, mapping, and the unstructured sources mined into reviewed, estimated facts.
  • Done when the target organization's ledger validates, then imports cleanly once 6.1 ships.

The findings model pays off even if no organization is ever harvested. The estate view is the block that decides whether the other three were worth doing.

Delivery · on the radar

On the radar: what the first cycle makes room for

Model

  • SBOM support. A build's component list enters through the same door as a scan; findings already carry the package coordinate that joins to it. Then "the component is gone" becomes distinguishable from "not scanned", and fan-out works by package as well as by CVE.
  • Acceptance stays read-only in Yontrack. The scanner-side files remain the source of truth; Yontrack reflects them, with their statement and expiry.

Producer

  • More collectors, added as organizations bring their tools.
  • A pull shipper: Yontrack fetching ledgers from a bucket or URL, once the file-based shippers have proven the contract.
  • A redaction layer, for the day a ledger crosses a trust boundary.
  • Packaging and distribution of the harvester.

Product

  • New readings, each added against a real use case rather than through a generic extension point.
  • Corrections in the ledger: retracting a fact rather than purging a source.
